The complete pathway

One threat. Two phases. A return to safety.

01

Stimulus

Frightening REM dream → perceived threat.

02

Nervous System

Amygdala alarm → hypothalamus command → sympathetic activation.

Locus coeruleus supports arousal.

03

Brain/Hormones

Spinal signals → adrenal medulla → adrenaline + norepinephrine.

HPA: CRH → ACTH → adrenal cortex → cortisol.

04

Response

Heart and breathing speed up. Pupils widen, digestion slows, muscles tense. Sweating and abrupt waking.

05

Behavior

Sit up, gasp, feel frightened, check the room.

06

Calming down

Recognize safety → vagal recovery → slower heart and normal breathing.

Hormones clear → baseline → sleep may return.

Neural signals and hormones overlap. Waking does not wait for cortisol. The HPA response is slower and varies; not every nightmare produces a cortisol surge.

Nerves react in seconds. Hormones keep you alert for hours.

A memory aid, not a stopwatch: longer-lasting hormonal effects are possible, not inevitable. A racing heart is mainly a fast autonomic effect, not proof of high cortisol.

Quick reference

Key terms, in plain English

Amygdala

Brain nuclei that help process emotional significance, including potential threats. “Alarm center” is a useful simplification.

Hypothalamus

A brain region coordinating automatic functions and hormonal signals, including the stress response.

Sympathetic nervous system

The autonomic branch that mobilizes fight-or-flight. Nerves carry electrical signals; chemical messengers act at their connections.

Parasympathetic nervous system

The autonomic branch supporting rest and digestion. Vagal endings release acetylcholine, helping slow the heart.

Adrenaline (epinephrine)

A fast-acting hormone released by the adrenal medulla to prepare the body for immediate action.

Cortisol

An adrenal cortex hormone that makes fuel available, including by raising blood sugar. Stress-related effects develop more slowly than adrenaline’s.

HPA axis

Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis: hypothalamus releases CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone); pituitary releases ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone); adrenal cortex releases cortisol.

REM sleep

Rapid eye movement sleep: active brain activity, vivid dreams and usually reduced skeletal muscle tone. Many nightmares occur during REM.

Locus coeruleus and norepinephrine

A brainstem nucleus and its alertness-related messenger. Normally largely silent in REM, renewed locus coeruleus activity helps waking. Norepinephrine also comes from many sympathetic endings and the adrenal medulla.

Pineal gland and melatonin

A small brain gland and its hormone signaling biological night. Melatonin supports sleep timing; it is not an on/off switch that keeps a person asleep.

Scientific accuracy notes & sources

A possible pathway, not a diagnosis or six isolated switches. Brain regions do not literally turn on and off. Muscle tone returns on waking; prolonged confusion is less typical of nightmares. Digestion slows rather than shutting down, and dry mouth reflects altered salivary activity. Electrical impulses travel along spinal pathways; norepinephrine does not simply flow down the spinal cord.

Stress can disrupt sleep, but a nightmare does not reliably suppress melatonin. Pineal melatonin is normally promoted by sympathetic nighttime signaling. Toward biological morning, melatonin generally falls and cortisol rises; this is not proof of what caused any individual awakening.
